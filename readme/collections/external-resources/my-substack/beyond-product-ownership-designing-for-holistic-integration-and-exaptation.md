# Beyond Product Ownership: Designing for Holistic Integration and Exaptation

### How to prevent Conway's Law from fracturing technology infrastructure when shifting away from traditional project management.

[Anan](https://substack.com/@anan12)Aug 05, 2026

1\. The Nokia Failure

Nokia collapsed not from a lack of technology or resources, but from organizational paralysis. A complex matrix structure and misaligned management incentives created a culture of fear and internal competition. Instead of adapting to a market paradigm shift, teams prioritized their own survival, rigidly clinging to Symbian—a deeply fragmented, device-coupled architecture that could not compete with unified ecosystems like Android.

2\. Conway's Law on Organizational Structure

Conway’s Law dictates that organizations inevitably design software systems that mirror their internal communication structures and political boundaries. If an enterprise or government is organized into distinct, politically isolated silos, its technical architecture will inherently become disjointed, duplicated, and incompatible across those boundaries.

3\. Mechanical Sympathy vs. Conway's Law

"Mechanical sympathy" involves designing a system that is perfectly optimized for the inherent technical reality and efficiency of the underlying architecture. Conway's Law is a systemic problem because it actively subverts mechanical sympathy. Instead of designing a product based on what is objectively best for the overall system, the technical architecture is compromised to accommodate team boundaries, departmental politics, and localized module ownership.

4\. The Leap to "Build and Run" Ownership

GovTech’s current transition away from a "one-off project-delivery model to a continuous product-ownership model" represents a massive, necessary organizational leap. By cutting traditional vendor management roles and expanding in-house software engineering capabilities, teams are taking full end-to-end responsibility to design, build, run, and continuously improve their specific domains.

5\. Skepticism: A Big Leap, but a Small Win

While giving a team absolute ownership over building and maintaining a product accelerates localized delivery, it is arguably a small win compared to the massive scale of modern public sector challenges. True strategic and policy agility requires more than just efficient, localized code delivery. Solving engineering bottlenecks within individual modules does not automatically dissolve the overarching political complexities or ensure that the government can pivot holistically during a crisis.

6\. Direction, Alignment, and Exaptation

To prevent these newly autonomous product teams from mutating into aggressive political silos (like Nokia's competing business units), strict incentive alignment is critical. Teams must be structurally incentivized to prioritize overarching integration over their specific module's survival. Crucially, leadership must enforce the capacity for exaptation—the willingness to transform, repurpose, or ruthlessly abandon and decommission existing products when they no longer serve the broader strategic ecosystem. If teams are only incentivized to build and protect their own modules, overall strategic agility will remain impossible.

7. Conclusion

Ultimately, escaping Conway’s Law requires decisive, unifying leadership. When Steve Jobs returned to Apple, he ruthlessly canceled dozens of projects, much to the dismay of teams whose passions and localized incentives were deeply tied to those specific products. Teams rarely self-align with a macro-vision organically; therefore, leadership's core function is to actively set—and when necessary, dissolve—that overarching direction.

However, top-down direction only succeeds if personnel at all levels possess the skills, values, and conceptual models to accurately translate the broader vision into their localized strategies. Crucially, traditional monetary remuneration must not obstruct this flexibility. If financial incentives are tied solely to the survival of a specific module, teams will defend it at the expense of the organization. Instead, as the open source community demonstrates, true strategic alignment and a willingness to adapt are best powered by intrinsic incentives: professional pride, peer recognition, and a shared dedication to the integrity of the broader ecosystem.
