# Education Measures Recall, Not Reasoning

### Do we have an education system or an examination system?

[Anan](https://substack.com/@anan12)Apr 01, 2026

> Do we have an education system or an examination system?

Most education systems do not test what they claim to produce. They claim to produce capable graduates. What they actually test is the ability to memorise and reproduce information under timed conditions. The distinction matters because the people who pass through these systems go on to occupy positions of authority and make decisions that affect everyone else.

## **The A-Level Hypothesis**

Consider a national examination system where university entry depends on a Z-score calculated from three mainstream subjects. The system also includes a general paper and an English paper, but these carry no weight in the Z-score calculation.

A testable hypothesis: students who score in the 90th percentile on mainstream subjects (mathematics, physics, chemistry) are likely to perform at the 50-60th percentile on the general paper. The general paper could be gamed too, but since it carries no Z-score weight, the incentive to invest in it is zero. The low score reveals where the system told students not to allocate effort. Conversely, students who perform well on the general paper tend to score in the 70-80th percentile across all subjects: consistently competent, but not exceptional in any single one.

If this pattern holds, the system is selecting for one kind of ability (recall under pressure) while filtering out another (reasoning under ambiguity). The students who enter university, and later industry and governance, are disproportionately those who excel at the first. The second group, the reasoners, either fall below the cutoff or proceed without recognition of their actual strength.

This is not a claim about intelligence. Both groups are capable. The claim is about selection: the system measures one thing and rewards it, while the thing it claims to value (the ability to think) goes unmeasured and unrewarded.

But selection is only half the damage. The other half is character formation.

The student who tops the Z-score has learned, through years of reinforcement, that the goal is not to understand but to optimise for marks. Which questions to attempt, which to skip, which topics yield the highest return per hour of memorisation, how to reverse-engineer the marking scheme. This is not learning. It is optimisation for the metric the system rewards. The student is admitted. The pattern is reinforced.

By the time they enter industry and then positions of authority, the character that was shaped is not “curious” or “rigorous” or “wise.” It is “optimise for the metric, find the shortcut, game the rules.” The general paper requires original argument and critical reasoning. It could be prepared for, but there is no incentive: it carries no weight in the Z-score. So the student, trained by the system to allocate effort where it yields marks, rationally ignores it. That is why the same student scores 50-60% on it. The score is not a measure of inability. It is a measure of where the system told them not to invest. Those who do well on the general paper did not follow the incentive. They learned to think because something outside the system drove them to. The system does not select for them.

So the examination system does two things simultaneously: it selects for recall over reasoning, and it creates incentives to exploit rules rather than understand principles. The first produces people the system never required to see the layer above. The second shapes behaviour toward asking “how do I optimise for this?” rather than “how do I improve it?” Both traits are reinforced for over a decade before the student enters the workforce. The outcomes that follow are not surprising. They are the system working as designed.

## **The Naturalist and the Examinees**

Imagine two biology students in the same cohort. One scores exceptionally on every examination. The other scores adequately but spends time outside the syllabus: reading library books, identifying species in the field, contributing observations to databases of endangered and locally found species.

Years later, the first has a degree and a career built on the credential. The second is the only person from that cohort who can identify a plant by its biological name in real time, the only one contributing to national biodiversity records. The examination was not designed to distinguish between them. It ranked the memoriser higher because that is what it was built to measure.

This is not an edge case. It is the predictable outcome of a system that tests recall and rewards gaming. The person who memorised the taxonomy for the exam and the person who learned it by walking through forests are not the same. The system treated them as if they were, and ranked the wrong one higher.

## **From Classroom to Workplace**

The same pattern reproduces in professional environments. Consider a software development team where several members hold industry certifications: cloud platforms, container orchestration, operating systems. These credentials were obtained to pass resume filters and interviews, which is rational behaviour within the system.

Now consider what happens when the team encounters a genuine modernisation need: migrating a build system, introducing continuous integration, adopting version control workflows, establishing automated testing.

This is where the examination system’s damage becomes visible. It does not produce people who ask questions. It produces people who answer pre-selected questions to score marks. When someone does ask a question, the response is binary: know or don’t know. There is no training in extracting models from reality, in looking at a running system and asking “what is this system not doing that it should be?”

So the modernisation need is not seen in the first place. The certified team member does not look at a 90-minute build and ask “why?” because the examination system never required them to question what was already working. And even if someone does see it, the response is structural: “that is not my job scope.” In a complex domain, where cause and effect are visible only in hindsight, how do you define a job scope? The answer, for the examination-system graduate, is simple: I was hired to complete tasks, so I will do whatever marks the task as done. Whether the task actually solves the problem or reaches the goal is not the question they were trained to ask.

In many organisations, the person who proposes and delivers modernisation is not the one with the most certifications. The certified colleagues passed the exam. The contributor understood the problem.

This is not about who is smarter. It is about what the certification measured and what the team and company incentivised. If it measured the ability to recall configuration parameters under exam conditions, it produced people who can pass exams. If the work requires the ability to see a 600-module system as a whole, identify where the build pipeline breaks, and propose an architectural change that no one asked for, the exam did not test for that.

## **The Selection Funnel**

The pattern compounds across layers:

1. The education system selects for recall and incentivises gaming
2. The selected enter industry with credentials that signal competence
3. The credentialed advance through organisational structures that reward visible compliance and metric optimisation
4. Those in authority make decisions based on the mental models the system taught them, optimising for measurable outputs rather than questioning whether the measures are right
5. The decisions are rational within the layer they see

No individual in this chain is acting irrationally. Each is optimising within the system they were trained to navigate. The problem is not the people. The problem is that the selection system never tested for the ability to see the system itself.

A person who can see one layer above the system they operate in, who asks not just “how do I win this game?” but “who designed this game and what does it optimise for?”, is not a product of the education system. They are a product of accident: wartime disruption, self-directed reading, entrepreneurial failure, or simply an inclination that the system had no mechanism to detect.

## **The Consequence**

When those who reason occupy the same level as those who recall, but the organisational structure gives authority based on credentials and tenure, the reasoner’s insights are structurally disadvantaged. They propose changes that the system has no category for. The credentialed majority, acting rationally, resist what they cannot evaluate.

The outcomes that follow, architectural decisions driven by technical limitations rather than strategic intent, governance frameworks that measure activity rather than impact, procurement systems that reward inputs rather than outcomes, are not failures of individuals. They are the predictable output of a selection system that never tested for the capability it most needs.

The education system is the root. Everything downstream, the workplace, the institution, the governance model, inherits its selection bias.

Scale this up and the consequences are no longer organisational. They are national. When an entire generation passes through a system that selects for recall over reasoning, the people who occupy positions of fiscal authority, regulatory oversight, and economic policy are, by statistical weight, those who were never trained to question the structural assumptions they inherited. The system equipped them to execute policy, not to evaluate whether the policy’s model still matches reality. It trained them to manage within a paradigm, not to recognise when the paradigm itself is failing. The economic collapse of a country is not one bad decision. It is thousands of structurally predictable decisions made by people the system selected precisely because they would not question the layer above.

This is not an indictment of a population. It is a diagnosis of a selection mechanism. The same people, differently selected, differently trained, would produce different outcomes. The variable is not the people. The variable is the system that decides who gets to decide.

Fixing it requires not better exams, but a different definition of what “capable” means. Until then, the system will continue to produce graduates who can recall everything except how to think about the system they are in.

## **Conclusion**

A system is defined by its goal. If the goal is marks, the system produces mark-scoring behaviour. If the goal is understanding, it produces thinking. We called ours an education system. We measured it by examination results. We got what we measured.

The system selected for marks. Mark-selection trained gaming. Gaming led to authority. Authority optimised for metrics. The metrics drifted from reality. The economy followed.

No one in this chain acted irrationally. Everyone optimised for exactly what the system rewarded. The tragedy is not that the system failed. The tragedy is that it succeeded, at producing exactly what it was designed to produce.

This diagnosis applies to everyone shaped by the system, including the person making the diagnosis. The structural patterns are easier to see than to escape.

The question is not how to fix the people. The question is whether we have the courage to redesign what the system selects for. Because the current system does not select for that courage either.
