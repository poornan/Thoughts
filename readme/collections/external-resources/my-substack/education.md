In Sri Lanka, “kuppi” is a popular campus term for informal, student-led study groups where senior or academically stronger students teach and guide their peers through difficult course modules.

What is a Kuppi?

• Meaning: The word traditionally translates to a small oil lamp or vial in Sinhala and Tamil, metaphorically shedding “light” on difficult subjects.

In the contemporary university sub-culture “kuppiya” is the name used for a student more intelligent/informative then the rest. (Kuppiya stands for brightness.) The classes are known as “kuppi classes” because they are conducted by the “kuppiyas”

• Function: Students organize these sessions—often in hostels or empty lecture halls—to fill gaps left by formal lectures, review past papers, prepare for vivas, and break down complex engineering, medical, or science modules.

They are classes conducted immediately prior to examinations, by the members of the same batch (rather than seniors) who have studied and confident with their knowledge for the benefit of the others. The objective of these classes are not to “teach” anything as such, but to fill the minds of the students with some information (which most of the times they may not even understand) so that they can go to the examination and write something to get a simple pass. For them, subject matters are something to be studied (mugged) for the exams and to be forgotten the day after.

• Culture: It is a core pillar of Sri Lankan university subculture, relying on peer familiarity, shared social bonds, and collaborative survival under high-pressure academic conditions.

It is not unnatural for Sri Lankan university lecturers to “leak” more than half of the question paper before the exams. (usually there is a special session meant for this immediately before the exams) Lecturers have to do this because otherwise the students might not score well and that will badly reflect on the former. Sometimes “kuppi classes” are meant to discuss these questions.

**How the Kuppi “Games the System”**

* **Targeted “Spotting”:** Instead of teaching the whole syllabus, a kuppi often focuses strictly on “spotting” predictable questions based on the last five years of past papers.
* **The “Word-by-Word” Blueprint:** Seniors often pass down the exact phrasing, specific keywords, or lecture note structures that a particular professor looks for when grading. Students are taught to memorize and recite these exact blocks of text to maximize marks.
* **Algorithmic Survival:** It treats the exam like an algorithm to be cracked rather than a measure of understanding. If a student can mimic the marking scheme perfectly, they pass, even if they forget the material two weeks later.

**Why This Happens: A Faulty System**

The authors of _Kuppi Talk_ and other critical educators argue that this “gaming” is a symptom of a larger structural failure within Sri Lankan universities:

* **The Lecture Hall Factory:** When formal lectures consist of a professor reading off slides to 300 passive students, real learning is already dead. The kuppi simply mechanises the next step: passing the test.
* **Assessment Failure:** If an exam can be defeated by word-for-word recitation, the flaw belongs to the examiners and the curriculum, which fail to test analytical or real-world problem-solving skills.

**Objectivity's Blind-Spot: The Dunning-Kruger Effect**

Dunning and Kruger’s research was inspired by the curious incident of Pittsburgh’s “lemon thief,” McArthur Wheeler, who, in broad daylight and without any disguise whatsoever, robbed not one but two banks. Thanks to surveillance footage, the police were able to identify and apprehend him quite quickly after the robberies. Curiously, Mr. Wheeler was very surprised about being caught, stating that he had, after all, “worn the juice!”
