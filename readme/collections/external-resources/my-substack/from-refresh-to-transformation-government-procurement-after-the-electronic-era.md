# From Refresh to Transformation: Government Procurement After the Electronic Era

### Singapore is refreshing GeBIZ. The larger opportunity is to change its structure, not only its features.

[Anan](https://substack.com/@anan12)Jul 09, 2026

More than $30 billion of government procurement runs through GeBIZ each year. The system is being refreshed, with the stated aim of a more transparent, efficient and user-friendly marketplace. The GeBIZ X team has been co-located so that business and technology staff work together. The direction is sound.

The refresh should be judged by what it is for. Public consultation is part of it and is necessary, but it has a known limit, caught in the line often attributed to Henry Ford: ask people what they want and they will say a faster horse. Consultation surfaces improvements to the system people already use, not the structural change they have not seen. The engine is that structural change, the part consultation cannot ask for: a system built for the digital era rather than a faster version of the electronic one. Identifying it is the work of domain experts, and the relevant domain is not procurement alone. It is digital governance.

**From electronic to digital.** The “e” in GeBIZ stands for electronic. The platform was built for the early-Internet era and did that job: it moved government business online. The technology base has since changed. Cloud computing, the Internet of Things, and robotic process automation should be designed in from the start, not added to an electronic system later. Moving from electronic to digital, GeBIZ to GdBIZ, is not an in-place upgrade but a different system.

**Procurement as core infrastructure.** Procurement is currently a standalone portal, integrated with other systems after the fact. It should instead be a core node in a connected, distributed national system, linked to national trade and stockpiling. For the state to see and move supply at scale, whether in a supply shock or in routine planning, integration should be part of the design rather than added later.

**Policy-based evaluation.** An electronic system encodes fixed rules. A digital system should be configurable by policy. Evaluation is one example: methods such as the Analytic Hierarchy Process and the Analytic Network Process should run inside the system, with policy selecting which applies to each procurement, rather than being done outside in offline spreadsheets.

**Demand aggregation.** This is not a new idea for GeBIZ. Using its transaction data, the Ministry of Finance ran public-sector demand aggregation across 18 procurement categories in 2004, for around $15 million in potential annual savings (”GeBIZ: From Vision to Reality”, DSTA, 2006). Government is the only buyer today, but the same platform could extend that to corporate buyers and, in time, partner governments, combining demand to obtain better terms. The 2006 paper’s other direction was to licence GeBIZ abroad as a product; licensing earns revenue but does not aggregate demand. Aggregation is the larger and different opportunity: it would make Singapore the point at which regional demand is pooled, rather than a supplier of procurement software.

GeBIZ succeeded at the task of its time, and that task has changed. A system built to make procurement electronic can now make it digital, and can position Singapore where the next model of public procurement is developed. That is a change of structure, not a refresh, and it is worth stating before the design is settled.

_I have worked on government e-procurement since 2015 and am glad to discuss the structure with anyone shaping it._
