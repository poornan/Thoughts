# Misconceptions from unaccounted differences of time and place

### Anchoring against our own experience benchmark fundamentally creates misconceptions because of time and space differences unaccounted.

[Anan](https://substack.com/@anan12)Sep 01, 2026

I think in 2015, I randomly picked up a book at Changi Airport on my way home for the holidays. It happened to open doors to many other books and was one of the essential strands in my quest for answers: The Art of Thinking Clearly by Rolf Dobelli. The more important part was his introduction, which explained where and when to use this book because it requires System 2 thinking. As I remember, it was mentioned that the fallacy of confirmation bias is described as the "mother of all misconceptions" because it is our tendency to interpret new information to fit existing beliefs while ignoring contradictory evidence.

Recently, I was contemplating probably a sub-context of confirmation bias and the anchoring effect, that we mostly make ourselves a sticky baseline. For example, when I am 10 years old and another is five, he is much younger (50%). Then, when I am 50, I am still thinking that the other one is half as young, though in reality, only 10% younger. This specific anchoring effect creates a lot of issues, mainly in the modern world, where experience has a different meaning, not calculated in years but paths. So, trying to understand another person by anchoring against our own experience benchmark fundamentally creates misconceptions because of time and space differences ignored.

> The taxonomy: this is the fixable part. What you're describing is not really a sub-context of confirmation bias, and it isn't classic anchoring (Tversky–Kahneman anchoring is about irrelevant numbers contaminating estimates). It is a documented, named phenomenon: egocentric anchoring and adjustment — Epley, Keysar, Van Boven & Gilovich (2004), Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 87, 327–339. People adopt others' perspectives by serially adjusting away from their own, and adjustment stops as soon as a plausible answer is reached — which is systematically short of the correct answer. Adjacent named cousins: false-consensus effect, curse of knowledge, projection bias.
