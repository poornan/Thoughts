# The origin and transmission of zero

### : a corrected note.

[Anan](https://substack.com/@anan12)Jun 24, 2026

_Definitions._ “Zero” has three senses, which must be kept apart: the **placeholder** (a mark for an empty position), the **written symbol**, and the **number** (a quantity with its own arithmetic).

_Origin._ The placeholder arose independently in several cultures — Babylon, and the Maya by the 4th century CE. The number did not. It was defined in India: Aryabhata (c. 500 CE) established the decimal place-value system that made a zero structurally necessary but used no symbol for it; Brahmagupta, in 628 CE, at Bhillamāla (modern Bhinmal), capital of the Gurjara kingdom under King Vyāghramukha, first treated zero as a number and stated its arithmetic. The earliest dated zero symbol is the Gwalior inscription of 876 CE.

_Transmission outward._ The decimal-and-zero system, together with Brahmagupta’s astronomy, passed from India to the Abbasid court around 771 CE, carried via Sindh — then under Arab rule since 712 CE — and rendered into Arabic as the _Zīj al-Sindhind_. The name is an Arabisation of the Sanskrit _siddhānta_ (”treatise”), not of the toponym Sindh, though the text did transit that province. The Persian mathematician al-Khwarizmi (c. 820 CE) was the pivotal transmitter, not the inventor; Arabic texts called the system “Indian reckoning” for roughly five centuries. It reached Europe in 1202 through Fibonacci.

_A complicating layer: two kinds of “astrology.”_ Indian _jyotiṣa_ has two branches of opposite pedigree. The calendrical-mathematical branch (_gaṇita_, the _siddhānta_ tradition, the _nakṣatra_ lunar mansions) is Vedic-internal. The horoscopic branch (_horā_ — birth charts, the twelve zodiac signs, the ascendant) is a Hellenistic import of the 2nd–3rd centuries CE, transmitted from Alexandria through the _Yavanajātaka_ and the _Pauliśa_ and _Romaka Siddhāntas_, and Sanskritised by Brahmanical scholars. The system that taught the Arabs therefore already carried Greek content.

_The deeper question: where the Vedic tradition itself belongs._ The Vedic tradition is one strand of Indian civilisation, not its whole, and is not primordially autochthonous. Its body was composed in the north-west subcontinent (the Sapta Sindhu); its Indo-Aryan language layer arrived from the Central Asian steppe in the second millennium BCE (steppe ancestry appears in South Asia after 2000 BCE, increasing after 1500; the Indus Valley population carried none). Vedic is, precisely, Indo-Iranian localised in India.

_The corrected India–Persia bond._ The real link between India and Persia is not in medieval mathematics — there the flow ran India to Persia — but at the Indo-Iranian root. Vedic Sanskrit and Avestan are sister languages, convertible by the regular _s → h_ sound-law: _soma_/_haoma_, _asura_/_ahura_, and — decisively — _sindhu_/_hindu_. “Hindu” is the Iranian pronunciation of “Sindhu.” The bond is genuine; it sits at the root, c. 2000 BCE, not at the zero.
