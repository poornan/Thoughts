# The Wrong Legal Form for Sri Lanka’s Digital State

### Sri Lanka has placed its national digital-transformation body inside a government-owned private company rather than a statutory one.

[Anan](https://substack.com/@anan12)Jul 05, 2026

_Sri Lanka has placed its national digital-transformation body inside a government-owned private company rather than a statutory one. Whether it is bound to serve the public turns on its founding Articles, which have not been published; and every state it holds up as a model kept that core function in a public body._

Sri Lanka is winding up the Information and Communication Technology Agency (ICTA) and replacing it with GovTech Sri Lanka (Pvt) Ltd, a wholly government-owned private limited company under the Ministry of Digital Economy.\[1] Policy will sit with a separate Digital Economy Authority, still at draft-legislation stage; cyber security with a dedicated regulator. GovTech is the delivery arm. It has spent recent months recruiting a full executive suite, with Dr Hans Wijayasuriya as chairman and Shevan Goonetilleke as Chief Executive Officer from 19 August 2026.\[2]\[3]

Most commentary has fixed on the people: are they capable, were they chosen well. That is the wrong level of analysis. The question worth asking is the legal form itself. Why is the body meant to build the nation’s digital public goods constituted as a “Pvt Ltd” at all?

The words used for institutions are not interchangeable; each denotes a specific legal status. “Authority” signals statutory power to bind others. “Board” signals a public body running a public function under public-law accountability. “Private limited company” signals shareholders, share capital, and, by default, the logic of returns. A state that chooses the company form to hold identity infrastructure and interoperability standards owes an explanation better than convenience.

The mismatch is not hypothetical. GovTech’s own homepage calls it “the Government’s lead implementation agency”, while its legal identity, further down the same page, is “Pvt Ltd”.\[4] In public-administration usage “agency” connotes a body established under public law, inside the accountability perimeter of procurement rules, the Auditor-General, and right-to-information. A company carries none of that by default. Each word is defensible alone, but the salient one, “agency”, implies an accountability the company form does not provide.

## The incentive problem, not an accounting contradiction

The founding language combines two claims that sit awkwardly together. GovTech, officials say, will be a state-owned enterprise operating “without the goal of generating commercial profit”, while meeting its operating costs through revenue from its own services.\[1] As accounting, that is not self-contradictory. It is cost recovery: revenue equals cost, surplus is zero, and utilities and statutory bodies run this way everywhere.

The problem is not the arithmetic. It is the gap between two things the sentence conflates: where the money comes from, and what the body optimises for. The profit disclaimer is a claim about the second. The self-funding model is a claim about the first, and a self-funding source comes to determine the objective it was meant only to fund. A body whose survival depends on what it can sell will, over time, optimise for what sells, whatever its charter says. The contradiction is not logical but incentive-based, and an incentive persists where a disclaimer does not.

There is a further reason this kind of good is ill-suited to this model. Digital identity, interoperability, a citizen platform: these are non-excludable network goods. Their value lies in universal, free-at-point-of-use access and the network effects that follow. Collecting service revenue requires excluding non-payers, which reduces the universality that gives the infrastructure its worth. For a public good, a cost-recovery model does not merely risk commercial behaviour; it works against the nature of the good.

One precision, because the loose version of this critique invites an easy rebuttal. Revenue-generating is not the same as market-driven. A monopoly provider charging captive agencies faces no competition, no customer exit, no price discovery, and so is not operating in a market. What it has is revenue-dependence, which produces commercial behaviour with no market to discipline it. The accurate charge is not that GovTech will be market-driven, but that it will be revenue-dependent, and revenue-dependence moves an institution toward commercial ends over time regardless of the profit clause. That charge does not rest on the disclaimer being insincere. It rests on the funding structure making the disclaimer unenforceable.

## The legal form other states chose

If Sri Lanka is modelling itself on anyone, it is the states whose digital government is held up as the standard. The legal form each gave to the body that builds the core is worth examining. The pattern is not subtle.

Singapore delivers through GovTech, a statutory board under the Ministry of Digital Development and Information.\[6] It is funded by appropriation and engages the market by buying: it calls open tenders and contracts vendors. It does not sustain itself by selling its platforms back to the agencies that depend on them. The form fixes the public mandate in statute; the funding keeps the body’s survival independent of its sales.

Singapore’s model also answers the obvious rejoinder, that it charges too. It does: businesses integrating Singpass and Myinfo pay per transaction above a free tier.\[5] But the charge falls on commercial re-users, principally banks, insurers, and fintechs, not on citizens; the core stays free at the point of use; the fees arrived only in 2022, after near-universal adoption, not to fund construction; and, decisively, the operator is appropriation-funded, so the revenue is incremental to a guaranteed budget rather than the basis of survival. Charging is not the distinction that matters. Depending on the charge for survival is. Singapore charges and does not depend; GovTech’s stated model depends.

Singapore also shows what a government company is properly for. NCS, once the State’s National Computer Systems, was corporatised in 1996 and is now a Temasek-linked commercial firm, held through Singtel, that competes for public and private contracts across Asia against Accenture, Infosys, and other integrators, including for Singapore government work.\[7] But NCS is a vendor, not the mandate-holder. The identity infrastructure, the interoperability standards, and the public mandate stay with GovTech, the statutory board; NCS sells services into that system from outside it. Singapore keeps the commercial company and the public mandate in two separate entities. It keeps more than one such company, in fact. ST Engineering, also Temasek-controlled and listed on the exchange, competes worldwide in defence, aerospace, and engineering.\[8] When Singapore procured the ERP 2.0 road-pricing system, the contract was contested between NCS and ST Engineering’s electronics arm and awarded by the Land Transport Authority, the statutory body that holds the mandate: two state-linked companies competing, a statutory authority deciding, the commercial layer and the mandate layer kept separate.\[9] Sri Lanka has put both layers in one. The objection was never that the state may not own a company that competes in the market; it is that this particular company holds the one function that must not be commercial.

The United Kingdom is even further from the company form. The Government Digital Service, which built GOV.UK and now the GOV.UK One Login identity platform, is not a company, nor even an arm’s-length body. It is a unit inside a department, currently the Department for Science, Innovation and Technology, staffed by civil servants and funded from the departmental budget.\[11] Reorganisation moves it between departments; it is not converted into a profit-capable company. The core of the digital state stays inside the civil service, under the civil service’s accountability rules.

Estonia, the most cited case, is the most instructive. Its infrastructure is run by the Information System Authority (RIA), a state authority under the economics ministry, which built and operates X-Road, the electronic identity, and the online voting system. The central good, the X-Road data-exchange layer, is released as open source under a permissive licence and is free to adopt. That is why roughly two-thirds of the organisations using Estonia’s X-Road are private companies, using a public facility they never had to buy, which is a public good functioning as one. Where Estonia needed a dedicated body to steward the shared X-Road core across borders with Finland and later Iceland, it did not incorporate a company. It created the Nordic Institute for Interoperability Solutions, a non-profit association jointly funded by the member governments.\[12]

None of this makes Estonia a utopia. Scholars have called parts of the story a façade and noted that some celebrated savings were redistributed rather than eliminated. But that criticism concerns outcomes and marketing, not form, and on form the point is not in dispute: the core is a state authority, the interfaces are open and free, and the dedicated steward is a non-profit.

So the honest reading of the comparators is not “everyone builds a statutory board, therefore Sri Lanka must”. The forms genuinely vary: a board in Singapore, a ministry unit in the United Kingdom, a state authority plus a non-profit association in Estonia. But in every case the mandate-holding core sits in a public-law or non-profit body, never in a company that depends on selling its services. Where a commercial government-linked company exists, as with Singapore’s NCS, it is a separate vendor kept apart from the core. Sri Lanka, alone among the models it invokes, has placed the core mandate itself inside a commercial company.

## Why not a board, and what incorporating a company avoids

The obvious retort is that a Sri Lankan statutory board could not have staffed itself. A board is bound by the Establishments Code and public-sector pay scales and cannot pay a senior architect a market wage. GovTech’s whole visible purpose is to hire exactly those people. So the company form is chosen to escape the pay code, which is also why ICTA ran as a company for two decades.\[15]

True, but note what that concedes. The pay ceiling is not fixed or inevitable. It is a policy the government wrote and can rewrite, as Singapore did in building flexible schemes into its public service so a statutory board could pay near-market rates. The honest diagnosis is therefore not “boards cannot work” but “Sri Lanka underpays its public technical staff and has not built the schemes to fix it”. Same symptom, different root, and only one of them is inherent.

That distinction exposes the actual choice. There were two ways to solve the pay problem. Reform the board framework so a statutory body can pay properly while keeping its entrenched public mandate: hard, slow, and requiring legislation. Or exit the framework by incorporating a company: fast, and escaping the pay code at once. Sri Lanka took the second. Routing around a constraint is not the same as fixing it. GovTech gets its salaries, every other board stays constrained, and the precedent is set that the way to get capable people into the public sector is to leave the public sector’s rules. That is the opposite of institutional repair.

The cost of the shortcut is the set of controls given up in taking it. The public-law obligations a statutory body carries, central procurement rules, the Auditor-General’s audit, parliamentary oversight, and right-to-information coverage, are precisely the constraints that make self-dealing hard. A government-owned company sits outside or at the margin of several of these by default. The same constraints that slowed hiring also slowed procurement abuse; remove them for one purpose and they are gone for the other. So the design does not merely permit corruption; it invites it. A body that strips out the controls which make self-dealing hard, while handling large public contracts, has built both the opportunity and the incentive for misappropriation into its own structure. This is a claim about the system, not about the people in it. In a complex adaptive system, the behaviour that emerges is a property of structure and incentives, not of the character of whoever occupies the roles, so a structure that removes the checks on self-dealing will tend to produce self-dealing whoever is in post. The design is the variable; individual virtue is not. That is precisely why this is not an allegation that the current appointees intend anything improper: the design is what remains after the founders have gone, and it is what the next occupants inherit. That the risk is real and not theoretical is shown by ICTA’s own history, with a parliamentary oversight committee flagging over one billion Sri Lankan rupees in losses on a single stalled initiative and another project reaching seventeen per cent of its plan.\[18]

## The deeper mismatch: wrong form for the kind of problem

A second objection underlies the first, and it is sharper. The argument so far concerns ownership and accountability. But the form also gets a prior question wrong: what kind of problem is this, and does the institution suit it.

Take Cynefin. Some problems are complicated: hard, but solvable by expertise and good practice. Building an identity API, an interoperability bus, or an authentication service once the pattern is known is complicated-domain work, and the right engineers will execute it. Other problems are complex: cause and effect are visible only in retrospect, and the way through is to run small, safe-to-fail experiments, keep what works, and drop what does not. Adoption, trust, sequencing, and whether a citizen platform is taken up in a low-trust, multilingual, post-conflict society are complex-domain work. Technical excellence cannot substitute for it, because the knowledge does not exist in advance; it must be discovered in the specific context.

Sri Lanka’s hard part is disproportionately complex, not complicated. The technology is largely solved and copyable. What is not copyable is whether it will be adopted here. That is exactly where a CV-based recruitment filter cannot detect the gap: it selects for people who know the answer, in a domain whose defining feature is that nobody knows the answer yet. It is not merely that CVs miss practical wisdom; the dominant problem type actively requires the probing, adaptive judgement that CVs do not measure and often select against. This is also why lifting Singapore’s design wholesale is a category error: transplanting a known good practice is a complicated-domain move, but complex systems are dispositional, and the same intervention produces different outcomes in different contexts. Singapore’s context is not Sri Lanka’s.

Here the company form is objectionable again, for a new reason. Complex-domain work runs on safe-to-fail experiments, which means the institution must be able to fail, but fail small, fail visibly, and be accountable for the learning. A private limited company is built to do the opposite: fail privately, with limited disclosure, outside the audit and information regime. So the form does not merely weaken accountability as a public-service value; it disables the feedback loop that complex-domain capability depends on. In this domain, scrutiny is not a constraint on delivery but part of the means of delivery. Concealing failure here is not discretion; it discards the only data available.

None of this makes Singapore’s structure a guarantee. Even with the right form, Singapore has miscategorised complex problems as complicated. In January 2024 the SimplyGo transition treated a change in how commuters see their fares and card balances as a routine systems migration; commuters rejected losing the balance display at the fare gate, and within two weeks the transport minister reversed the plan, committed S$40 million to keep the older cards running, and apologised.\[10] ERP 2.0, the satellite road-pricing system, was awarded to an NCS-led consortium in 2016 for S$556 million, promised for 2020, delayed to 2023 on a global microchip shortage, and rolled out from 2023 into 2026 without its central purpose: distance-based charging, the very capability that justified moving to a satellite system, was deferred, so the new system for now reproduces what the old gantries already did.\[9] The on-board unit also drew objection for its bulk and its necessity, but the primary failures were the years of delay and the undelivered core goal. Both are complex-domain failures: the engineering was buildable and was built; whether the public would accept the unit, and whether the distance-charging goal could survive contact with drivers and politics, was assumed rather than worked out. But consider the recovery. Singapore caught and corrected SimplyGo in two weeks, precisely because a transparent statutory body under public scrutiny cannot conceal a backlash. That is the feedback loop working, and it is exactly the loop a company built to fail privately would suppress.

The Sri Lankan record shows the same miscategorisation, but without the structure that let Singapore recover. It is there twice.

Take a service. The Registrar General digitised birth records and gave the consuming departments direct database access, yet the paper requirement survived. When I applied for a driving licence in 2018, I was required to produce a birth certificate certified within the past six months, to attest a birth that cannot change and that the state’s own database already held. In June 2022 the Registrar General formally directed that certified copies do not expire. As late as February 2026, the press was still documenting citizens queuing for hours at the Divisional Secretariat for a stamped copy, because Immigration and the Registration of Persons Department still demanded a six-month document, though both already had database access.\[13] Eight years, and the state’s own correction, and the requirement survived. The record was digitised, which is complicated and was done. The behaviour of the agencies that consume it was never changed, which is complex and was not attempted. And the paper step that survived is, not coincidentally, a chargeable one.

Take a policy. In 2022 the ICTA ran a public consultation on a National Policy for Digital Transformation of Education.\[14] Reviewing it at the time, I found that the document delivered none of what its title promised. Its objectives were tactical actions to make existing processes electronic. It never distinguished its three operative words. Digitisation changes the medium and leaves the process intact. Digitalisation streamlines an existing process with digital tools. Digital transformation redesigns the process, the model, and the institution around what digital makes possible. The document’s substance was the first, presented under the name of the third. Making a prevailing manual process electronic, without touching the process or the institution, is the automate-the-existing-process model of the National Computerisation Plan of the 1980s, offered four decades later under the vocabulary of transformation.

This is the failure mode Feynman termed cargo cult in 1974:\[19] the outward forms of a practice are reproduced faithfully while the one element that makes it work is absent, so the intended result does not follow. Here the outward form is reproduced, the label “transformation” and the electronic version of the service, while the transforming element, the redesign of process and behaviour, is absent. And the form being imitated is itself obsolete, so the imitation reproduces a forty-year-old model rather than current practice. My own view, from observing the sector, is that this describes much of Sri Lanka’s public-sector digital work, not only these two cases. But two cases are enough to make the structural point.

The deficiency, then, is not technical competence, which the state has repeatedly shown. It is the practical judgement, phronesis, to treat adoption, trust, and cultural change as the first-order problem rather than as something that follows automatically once the technology is delivered. That judgement is exactly what a CV filter does not measure, what a competence-based operating model does not serve, and what a company built to fail without disclosure cannot bring to light. This is why the institutional-form question and the epistemic question turn out to be the same question. The body must be built so that the judgement it most needs is selected for, exercised, and, above all, held up to scrutiny in public, because in a complex domain that scrutiny is not oversight of the work but part of the work itself.

## The requirement is entrenchment, not a board

Here the critique must be precise about its own claim. “It must not be profit-first” does not entail “it must be a statutory board”. It entails something narrower and more general: a non-distribution constraint, so no surplus can be paid to shareholders; a ring-fenced core, so essential infrastructure is appropriation-funded and cannot be turned into a revenue line; and a public-good mandate, all entrenched in the constitutive instrument so a future board cannot quietly amend it away. A company limited by guarantee delivers this by default. A plain company with bespoke, entrenched Articles can deliver it too, while keeping the hiring flexibility a board lacks.

GovTech has none of these by default. Its own registrar offers incorporation as a company limited by guarantee, the standard non-profit vehicle, and the state did not use it.\[16] GovTech is limited by shares, the profit-capable type. Under the Companies Act it may adopt off-the-shelf Model Articles, which carry no non-distribution clause, or file customised ones.\[17] So the whole question reduces to a single point: did GovTech file bespoke Articles that entrench the public mandate, or did it take the standard template? That document is not published. Everything else is inference until it is.

## The structural documents settle what the CVs cannot

This returns us to the people, and to why the debate about them cannot be settled the way both sides want.

The instinct to worry about the leadership is not baseless. A CV filter selects for technical competence, the skill of delivery. Building a public institution demands practical wisdom oriented toward the public good, a different faculty, and no CV screen tests for it. Worse, the professional formation may push the other way: a career spent optimising shareholder value trains the habit of metering, excluding, and monetising, the opposite of what a non-excludable public good requires. The concern is not that the appointees are unqualified, but that the filter measures technical skill and makes no attempt to measure the disposition the job most needs.

But two moves must be resisted, one in each direction.

The first is reading conviction into a resignation. It is tempting to treat a chairman leaving a well-paid regional telecom seat for a government role as a costly signal of public-spiritedness.\[2] It is not safe evidence. Any move into public office can look, on the salary line alone, like a sacrifice, because a public salary is often a fraction of a private one. But the salary difference is the wrong quantity. Proximity to the allocation of public resources and to the standard-setting that decides which vendors win is worth something a corporate seat is not, and whether it nets to more or less than the old package is unknowable from outside. Unknowable means it is not evidence.

The pattern is clearest in an example from elsewhere in the region. When a film star enters politics, the salary line suggests an enormous sacrifice, since a chief minister’s pay is a fraction of a screen income. Yet no one reads a chief ministership as a sacrifice, because the office commands power, patronage, and post-office wealth the payslip never shows. The salary delta is the wrong measure there too, and for the same reason.

The second, equal and opposite, is reading the absence of conviction off a boilerplate appointment notice. A press release is written to suppress the very signals one would look for; its silence proves nothing. And the new chief executive’s record, transforming an enterprise-technology firm and founding an industry product council, is building rather than caretaking.\[3] Building for profit is not building for the public good, but nor is it the empty manager the harsh reading imagines.

So the honest conclusion is more limited than either side wants. From outside, we cannot distinguish three people with the identical CV and the identical press release: the genuine convert who took a real net loss for the public good; the continuer who will monetise the same position in slower, legal, deferred forms, returning through the revolving door to a sector whose government spending he now shapes; and the case where the sacrifice narrative merely serves the government’s presentation. Nothing publicly available separates them. To convict on these documents is as unsound as to acquit on them.

There is one more point worth making, because it is the flaw underneath the whole complaint. To insist the situation demanded a rare, outsized figure, and that these appointees are not it, is, on the impartial-state logic the reformers themselves invoke, a concession that the institution has not been built. The point of an impersonal institution is that it does not depend on exceptional individuals. A body that works only when an exceptional person happens to be found is a fragile body. The deepest achievement of the leaders usually cited as models was not their own brilliance but the systems they left behind that never again required their calibre. An institution that must be staffed by an outlier is not a strong institution waiting for an exceptional leader; it is an institution not yet built, depending on an individual. And outliers, in any case, cannot be recruited: they emerge through founding moments and crises, never through a selection filter, because by definition they sit outside the distribution the filter was built to sort.

This is why the argument keeps returning from the individuals to the documents. Character inferred from a CV or a resignation cannot be verified in either direction. The only things that would discriminate between the convert, the continuer, and the presentation are structural and on paper: the non-distribution clause, the conflict-of-interest register, the recusal rules that separate a leader from decisions touching former vendors, and a selection process with a transparent basis. None of these requires judging anyone’s character. That is exactly why they are the things to demand, and exactly the things not yet shown.

Sri Lanka may well end up with a capable digital state. But capability delivered through an opaque process inside a profit-capable company does not build the accountable process, and does not make the company trustworthy after its founders leave. Singapore, the United Kingdom, and Estonia each chose a different legal form for the task, and each kept the core within the public-law or non-profit category. Sri Lanka chose the one form that did not. The mature test of an institution is whether it works under ordinary, competent, replaceable leadership. GovTech has so far been built in a way that will work only if the individuals in charge are exceptional. That dependence on exceptional individuals, rather than any judgement about whether these particular appointees are exceptional, is the evidence that the institution has not yet been built to last.
